The first in-house experiment we've run on human participants under our own IRB — evaluating real-time frontal-theta detection and low-latency Open-LIFU triggering during a 2-back working-memory task.

Primary hypothesis Frontal theta can be detected in real time reliably enough to trigger temporally contingent Open-LIFU.
Exploratory hypothesis Active Open‑LIFU triggering may produce short‑term, event‑locked changes in frontal theta power and measurable shifts in task performance.
Why LIFU

Other stimulation methods drown the EEG in artifacts. This one doesn't.

Four ways to stimulate the brain. Only one lets the EEG keep reading while it fires.

LIFU Acoustic (mechanical) wave ✓EEG stays clean. No electromagnetic interference, so the closed loop keeps reading in real time while it stimulates.
TMS High-intensity magnetic pulse ✕Blinds the EEG. Saturates nearby sensors, disrupting real-time monitoring during the pulse.
DBS Implanted high-frequency current ✕Contaminates the signal. Surgically implanted electrodes inject large artifacts directly into scalp recordings.
tDCS Continuous scalp current ✕Drowns out the signal. Polarizes the skin–electrode interface, causing persistent baseline drift.
Trigger biomarker

Frontal theta (4–7 Hz) midline power scales with working-memory load and is reproducible session to session.

Stimulation target

Left dlPFC via the F3 scalp site, the brain's working-memory hub.

Protocol & Pipeline Design

One clock. Four programs. Zero manual handoffs.

Every component talks over named Lab Streaming Layer streams instead of being hard-wired. Tap a stage to see how it works.

The g.tec BCI Core 8 streams 8 raw EEG channels out over LSL. g.Pype takes the lead channel through 60 Hz notch → 4–7 Hz theta bandpass → power extraction → 50-sample moving average → decimate to 25 fps, while all raw and processed channels are recorded natively. Output is a clean, individualized z-score baseline (μ, σ) from a 100-second resting calibration.

MAD-based artifact gating, then a six-condition AND gate must all read true before anything fires. This is the actual logic, running live on a simulated theta z-score:

ARMED
1 baseline ready AND 2 task active AND 3 z>1.5 AND 4 z<10 ceiling AND 5 cooldown clear AND 6 budget remains
Sonications fired: 0

Cooldown is compressed for this demo (5s). The real pipeline enforces 10–15s.

3D Slicer hosts a modified OpenLIFUSonicationControl module, the same interface used to register the MRI, plan focus depth and beam steering, and set the acoustic parameter set before a session ever starts. During the task, that module listens for the LSL threshold event and fires the transducer with the pre-planned sonication.

A continuous stream of visual stimuli appears one at a time. On each trial, the participant judges whether the current item matches the one shown two trials back. A classic working-memory probe that demands holding, updating, and comparing against a constantly shifting reference point. Difficulty, timing, and stimulus sequencing are held identical across active and sham blocks, so any behavioral difference reflects stimulation condition rather than task design.

Measured, not estimated

Software timestamps can drift from what the hardware actually did. So, we wired the transducer's own trigger line straight into the EEG.

To get a latency figure we could actually defend, not just a log entry. So we tapped Open-LIFU's own TTL trigger output and fed it directly into a spare channel on the EEG headset.

The catch: that line fires a 5V logic pulse, and an EEG front-end is built to read microvolts, not volts. We had to attenuate it down to roughly 100mV before it was safe to feed in, butstrong enough to register clearly on the channel without saturating it. That's how we measured latency directly off hardware, not inferred from software timestamps.

Session design

Two sessions per participant, ≥60 min apart, four blocks each (two active, two sham), counterbalanced:

Sequence A: Sham → Active → Active → Sham
Sequence B: Active → Sham → Sham → Active
Safety stop criteria

Immediate halt on reported pain, discomfort, dizziness, visual disturbance, neurologic symptoms, a request to stop, device malfunction, coupling failure, or any abnormal system behavior.

Results — 2 participants, pilot data

What we actually saw in the loop.

All findings below are descriptive, feasibility-level observations, not powered for statistical inference.

Key finding

The device artifact isn't just small; it's fully separable from theta via ICA.

Unfiltered, Open-LIFU's electrical footprint shows up as clean bands for the duration of the sonication window.

Post-hoc, the noise showed stereotyped, non-neural spatial patterns with most energy above the theta band. ICA and band-specific filtering removed it, with no residual artifact in theta. Although results are exploratory, they are consistent with LIFU introducing negligible EEG artifact relative to TMS, DBS, or tDCS. The artifact is filterable, not just small enough to ignore.

All active trials passed the online/offline trigger-agreement test with error under 5ms, or within a single EEG sample. End-to-end latency, read directly off the Ch8 TTL loopback:

σ = 36.5ms across all sonications, range 329–513ms.

Honest Limits

What n=2 can't tell you yet.

No skull correction: beam steering wasn't adjusted for individual bone thickness.
Scalp-based targeting: for non-MRI participants is less precise than structural guidance.
n=2: every physiological finding here is descriptive, not inferential.
MI capped at 0.4: this pilot, well inside the approved envelope, for tolerability.
Slicer path (~420ms): is the latency bottleneck; direct Python runs at ~170ms.
Intermittent UART faults: and rare high-voltage-without-sonication events required retries.
